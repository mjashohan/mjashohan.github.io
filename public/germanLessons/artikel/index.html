Die vier Fälle

What each case is for

German marks a noun's job in the sentence on the article, not on word order. Four jobs, four cases. Learn the question word for each — it is the fastest way to test a sentence you are writing.

CaseQuestionJob in the sentenceExample
Nominativ1st case Wer? Was? The subject — who does it. Also after sein, werden, bleiben, heißen. Der Kollege arbeitet. · Das ist ein Fehler.
Akkusativ2nd case Wen? Was? The direct object — the thing directly affected. Ich frage den Kollegen. · Ich sehe die Tür.
Dativ3rd case Wem? The indirect object — the receiver / beneficiary. Ich helfe dem Kollegen. · mit der Bahn.
Genitiv4th case Wessen? Possession and belonging; after a few prepositions. das Auto des Kollegen · während der Pause.

Word order does not decide the case

Den Film hat der Junge gesehen. — still "the boy saw the film". den marks the object wherever it stands.

This is why the endings matter more than position: you can move a phrase to the front for emphasis without changing who did what.

Two objects: dative before accusative

Ich gebe dem Kind das Buch. Dat → Akk

With verbs like geben, schenken, zeigen, erklären, schicken, bringen, empfehlen: the person is dative, the thing is accusative.

If the thing is a pronoun, it jumps in front: Ich gebe es dem Kind.

The three shortcuts that save the most time

1 · Only masculine changes in the accusative.
der → den. Feminine, neuter and plural are identical to the nominative. Half of all "case errors" disappear with this one rule.
2 · Feminine in the dative and genitive is always der.
Which is why der in a sentence is not automatically a masculine subject: Ich helfe der Frau.
3 · Masculine and neuter behave identically in dative and genitive.
dem / des for both. You only need to separate them in the nominative and accusative.

Words that follow the der-pattern exactly

Same endings as the definite article, in every case:

dieserjederjener manchersolcherwelcher alle (pl)beide (pl)

Learn -r / -e / -s / -e as the signal endings and you can build them all: dieser Mann, diese Frau, dieses Kind, diese Leute → accusative diesen Mann, dative diesem Mann.

Das Nomen im Fall

When the noun itself changes

Dative plural adds -n

mit den Kindern

aus den Häusern

bei den Kollegen

Skip it if the plural already ends in -n (die Frauen → den Frauen) or in -s (die Autos → den Autos).

Genitive m/n adds -s or -es

des Lehrers, des Fensters multi-syllable

des Mannes, des Kindes one syllable

-es is obligatory after -s, -ß, -z, -x, -tz: des Platzes, des Hauses. Feminine nouns never change.

N-Deklination: -n everywhere but nominative

A group of masculine nouns takes -(e)n in all cases except the nominative singular.

der Studentder Kollegeder Junge der Menschder Nachbarder Kunde der Herrder Name

Ich frage den Studenten / dem Studenten / des Studenten.

der Herr takes -n in the singular but -en in the plural: den Herrn, die Herren. der Name is irregular in the genitive: des Namens.

Die Auslöser

What forces which case

Two things decide the case: the verb and the preposition. A preposition always wins — once a noun sits behind one, that preposition dictates the case, regardless of the verb.

Accusative prepositions — always accusative

durchfürgegen ohneumbisentlang

Memory hook: DOGFUB — durch, ohne, gegen, für, um, bis.

Wir gehen durch den Park, ohne einen Plan.

entlang normally comes after the noun: die Straße entlang.

Dative prepositions — always dative

ausaußerbei gegenübermitnach seitvonzuab

Memory hook: aus–außer–bei–mit–nach–seit–von–zu chanted as one word is the classic classroom drill.

Seit einem Jahr wohne ich bei meiner Familie.

Contractions you will see constantly: zu dem → zum, zu der → zur, bei dem → beim, von dem → vom.

Dative verbs — no accusative object at all

These verbs take a dative object where English uses a direct object. Learn them as a list; there is no rule.

helfendankengefallen gehörenpassenpassieren schmeckenantwortenfolgen glaubengratulierenbegegnen fehlenwehtunzuhören vertrauenwidersprechenzustimmen ratenverzeihen

Das Hemd gehört dem Chef. Dat

Die Wohnung gefällt meiner Frau. Dat

With gefallen, schmecken, gehören, fehlen the German subject is the thing, not the person — that is why the verb agrees with the thing: Die Schuhe gefallen mir.

Genitive prepositions

währendwegentrotz (an)stattaußerhalbinnerhalb aufgrunddank

während der Besprechung · trotz des Wetters

In speech, wegen, trotz, während are very often used with the dative (wegen dem Wetter). Accepted colloquially, marked wrong in a B1 written exam — use the genitive when you write.

Possession in everyday speech usually replaces the genitive with von + dative: das Auto von meinem Bruder instead of das Auto meines Bruders. Both are correct; the genitive is the written register.

Die Wechselpräpositionen

The nine two-way prepositions

These nine take either case. The verb's meaning decides: movement toward a destination takes the accusative, position within a place takes the dative.

anaufhinter innebenüber untervorzwischen
Wohin? → Akkusativ

Ich stelle die Lampe auf den Tisch.

Wo? → Dativ

Die Lampe steht auf dem Tisch.

The verb pairs that give it away
Movement → AkkusativPosition → DativMeaning
legen (legte, gelegt)liegen (lag, gelegen)lay down / lie
stellen (stellte, gestellt)stehen (stand, gestanden)stand up / stand
setzen (setzte, gesetzt)sitzen (saß, gesessen)set down / sit
hängen (hängte, gehängt)hängen (hing, gehangen)hang up / be hanging
sich setzensein, bleibensit down / be, stay

Careful: this rule is about direction, not motion. Ich laufe im Park (dative — running around inside the park) vs. Ich laufe in den Park (accusative — running into the park). Both involve movement; only the second has a destination.

A handful of these prepositions are fixed with one case when used non-spatially: warten auf + Akk, denken an + Akk, sich freuen über + Akk, teilnehmen an + Dat, leiden unter + Dat. Learn these with the verb, not with the rule.

Die strukturellen Regeln

Three rules with no exceptions

The last word wins

In a compound noun — and German builds them constantly — the gender comes from the final element. Everything before it is just description.

die Hand + der Schuh → der Handschuh

das Haus + die Tür → die Haustür

die Arbeit + der Platz → der Arbeitsplatz

fahren + das Rad → das Fahrrad

This single rule resolves thousands of nouns. When you meet a long unfamiliar compound, chop off everything but the last chunk and ask its gender.

Every plural is die

Gender disappears in the plural — nominative and accusative are die, dative is den (+ noun -n), genitive is der.

der Mann → die Männer

das Kind → die Kinder

The cost of this is the reverse problem: a bare die is ambiguous between feminine singular and any plural. The verb form usually resolves it: Die Frau kommt vs. Die Frauen kommen.

The diminutive overrides everything

Add -chen or -lein and the noun becomes neuter — including for people.

die Frau → das Fräulein

der Mann → das Männchen

der Hund → das Hündchen

This is why das Mädchen is neuter: it is historically the diminutive of die Magd. Grammar beats biology. The vowel usually takes an Umlaut too: a→ä, o→ö, u→ü.

Endings that look like a rule but are not

-er is masculine for agents and tools, but die Mutter, die Butter das Fenster, das Wasser

-e is feminine ~90% of the time, but the weak masculines break it: der Name, der Junge, der Kollege, der Löwe

Ge- is neuter for collectives, but der Gedanke, der Geschmack die Geschichte, die Gefahr, die Geduld

How to learn a new noun so this stops being a problem

Never store a bare noun. Store the three-part unit — article, noun, plural — as one sound: die Wohnung, die Wohnungen · der Schlüssel, die Schlüssel · das Fenster, die Fenster. Better still, store it inside a short phrase you would actually say, because the phrase carries the case too: Ich suche meinen Schlüssel. A noun learned as a phrase never has to be reconstructed under time pressure in a speaking exam.
